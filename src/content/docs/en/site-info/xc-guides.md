El Peñón is one of the best cross-country sites in Mexico. From December to March, flights of over 100 km are possible, and records exceed 200 km. The classic flight goes to Valle de Bravo, landing at the Santa María lakeside landing zone, 14 km to the north-northwest.

:::caution
The heights given below are indicative: base your decisions on the day's conditions and on your own judgment. If you don't know the site, fly these routes with an [authorized guide](/en/lists/authorized-pilots/#guides) first. Before you go, check the [airspace and prohibited landing zones](/en/site-info/airspace/).
:::

## Landmarks

The main landmarks (they are all waypoints) on the way from El Peñón to Valle de Bravo:

- Launch and the El Piano landing zone
- El Peñón (the rock) and the Wall (Las Paredes)
- Peñitas, Sacamecate, San Agustín and La Casa
- Cerro Gordo, Escalerillas and Casas Viejas
- Santa María, the lakeside landing zone in Valle de Bravo

The other routes add G-Spot, Espinazo, Maguey, Tres Reyes and Divisadero to the west, and Quintanilla, Saucos and Mariposas (the Butterflies) to the east.

## Peñón–Valle via Peñitas

The easiest route to Valle de Bravo. It stays within glide of the main road: if you land short, you can catch one of the many collective taxis without walking far. With several good landing fields along the way, it's also the recommended route for pilots starting cross-country.

### Launch

On the morning flight, avoid taking off after 13:00: thermal cycles at launch typically build from light at 10:00 to strong by 13:00, and the wind picks up significantly in the afternoon as the valley wind comes in.

You'll often meet the full strength of the wind and thermals right after launch, so keep flying your glider (don't let go of your brakes) before settling into your harness or touching your instruments. Between strong cycles there are often lighter, launchable conditions; with pilots climbing out in front there's little risk of sinking out, but you need confidence and practice with forward or reverse launches.

### House thermal and Peñitas

The house thermal is to the left of launch, above a south-facing bowl and saddle. Climb at least 400 m above launch (less on a high-performance wing) to reach the valley behind it. Heading north behind launch, you'll see two rocks on your left (the Peñitas) and a hill straight ahead (Cerro de las Tres Cruces).

This valley is usually hard to climb in, especially early: don't go back behind launch before noon. Its advantage is the straight stretch of road, with collective taxis passing all the time, and two very large fields. The field farther from launch sometimes charges a small landing fee, but it has a windsock.

Take as much height as possible to arrive above the Peñitas, which usually work, and fly straight to them. If you arrive below them, you can try to soar the second Peñita, but recovering there is quite difficult. If you're too low to get over the Peñitas:

1. Try the thermal that usually comes off Cerro de las Tres Cruces.
2. Failing that, a bit farther on, a field with horseshoe-shaped stables often triggers a thermal (it can be rough).
3. The last option is a thermal at the end of the last field, but it isn't reliable, and if you drift too far downwind you can run out of landing options.

### Sacamecate

If you climb in any of these, the next step is Sacamecate. Depending on the day's wind, it usually works on its southwest face (where there's a small hut) or on its northeast face, toward the garbage dump (Basurero). If you're not climbing well and have the fields toward Jovan within glide, fly over the dump: it usually gives off a stinky thermal. If you climb well in any of these thermals, you'll often get higher than at launch and connect with the convergence.

### San Agustín or Jovan

From Sacamecate, take as much height as possible for the transition to Cerro San Agustín.

If you'd rather stay within reach of landings and the main road, you can head for Jovan, but that valley is difficult: thermals there aren't consistent, and you'll have a headwind from there to Valle. From Jovan, make sure you reach the Casas Viejas fields and try to climb there to get to Valle. Don't land at the Valle de Bravo aerodrome, in this area: it's a [prohibited landing zone](/en/site-info/airspace/#prohibited-landing-zones).

Via San Agustín, the convergence and thermal sources shift a lot with the day's wind strength and direction, so arrive high enough to search for the climb without committing to the Mesa. The convergence works better higher up; use the clouds as clues. San Agustín usually has no rotor, since the winds converge there.

The landings are to the northeast, but somewhat far from San Agustín, so keep an eye on your fields. If you don't mind a long walk, you can search lower over the Mesa south of San Agustín, where there are several landings, or at La Casa, the field below the hill just east of San Agustín. Its residents have sometimes been unwelcoming, but they have also driven pilots several kilometers to the main road.

### To Valle

With 1,000 m above launch at San Agustín, you can glide to Valle. But you'll reach Valle through the venturi, and a lower-performance wing may not make it. On the way you have the Casas Viejas fields, where it's very hard to climb (unless the convergence is much farther north than usual). Past Casas Viejas, the last field before Valle is Santa María del Pueblo.

If you're not high enough at San Agustín to reach Valle, jump to Escalerillas. As at San Agustín, thermals and convergence vary with wind strength and direction. Thermals come off both its south and north sides but are often hard to work low, until they join about 150 m above the ridge. Watch your landings: scratch too low here and you can get stuck. You have the fields on the Mesa (not recommended: a very long walk) and, to the northeast, those of Casas Viejas.

The convergence is usually between a stronger southwest wind from the Temascaltepec valley and a weaker northwest wind in the Valle valley. It often tilts north from the top of Escalerillas and San Agustín, so that pilots at the top of it may be almost over Casas Viejas.

The transition from Escalerillas to Valle is more crosswind and avoids the venturi at the downwind corner of the lake, so there's less risk of coming up short. With 800 m above launch, you have Valle on glide. Congratulations!

## Peñón–Valle via the Wall

Recommended for pilots with a little more experience: the thermals around the Peñón, the Wall and G-Spot are variable, the area can be rough, and the transitions are longer, with fewer landings. The landmarks for this flight are G-Spot, Espinazo, Maguey and Cerro Gordo.

### El Peñón

After climbing in the house thermal, fly toward the Peñón. If you leave with less than 300 m above launch, you may arrive low; you can usually soar the cliffs, but it can be turbulent and unpleasant, so glide to the Peñón with as much height as possible. If you leave with 500 m above launch, you'll arrive at the top of the Peñón, or at least the upper part of the rock (depending on wind strength).

There are several thermals at the Peñón:

- The first one usually drifts a lot toward the venturi between the Peñón and launch: be careful.
- The second usually comes up the south side of the cliffs, out of the bowl in the rock (la olla). It's turbulent and stuck to the rock.
- Out in front (south) of the Peñón, there are often smoother, wider thermals that climb as fast, on average, as the bullets near the cliffs. This more southerly line also avoids the venturi on the east side of the Peñón.

Some pilots prefer not to turn near the rock, and instead follow the spurs that come down from the Wall, or jump low to the Wall. There are thermals there, but if you don't find one it's a long glide to a landing. Once at the height of the Wall, you can ridge soar it and catch thermals as the ridge lift runs out; as in front of the Peñón, it can be turbulent. Thermals here often drift quickly back over the Mesa unless they're very strong, and if you lose a climb over the Mesa, it can be hard to get back against a strong inflow.

### G-Spot

Climb as high as possible before going to the G-Spot mesa: arriving low can mean severe turbulence, since the mesa gives off many thermals and winds converge from all sides. Ideally, arrive high, where the thermals have merged. On a very good day, if you climb enough at the Wall you don't need the G-Spot, but it's usually where the thermals go highest, so it's normal to use it for the next transition.

From the G-Spot, there are three options:

1. **Sacamecate or San Agustín**, then the [Peñitas route](#san-agustín-or-jovan). This crosses the Mesa: if you aren't very high, you need a climb before Sacamecate or San Agustín, or you'll land on the Mesa and have to walk, unless someone from the village gives you a ride down to the road (which sometimes happens).
2. **Straight to Cerro Gordo.** You need at least 1,200 m above launch: there are no climbs between the G-Spot and Cerro Gordo.
3. **Via the Maguey**, if you can't climb as high. 700 m above launch is a good height to arrive and search for thermals on the slopes of the Maguey. To make the crossing easier, climb toward or at Espinazo first, to get more upwind before crossing the valley.

### Maguey

The Maguey is a difficult point, generally somewhat turbulent because it's in a venturi. The hill rises from the Mesa, with spurs and bowls coming down to the southwest, where it usually works if you arrive lower than the hill. Don't scratch at the base of the hill: the wind there is usually accelerated, and you could end up landing and walking a long way.

If you're lower than the Mesa, the best option is the rock walls, but keep landing fields within reach: they're far, near San Pedro. If you climb enough on the walls, try the Maguey itself, where thermals vary a lot with the day's wind. With more than 700 m above launch at the Maguey, you can glide to Cerro Gordo. With less, it's tricky: there are no landings and usually no thermals between the two.

### Cerro Gordo

Cerro Gordo usually has no rotor. But if you come from the Wall or the G-Spot, don't go around its right (east) side unless you're higher than the hill: it usually doesn't work low on that side. Cerro Gordo sometimes works on one side and sometimes on the other.

Climb a little above Cerro Gordo and your arrival in Valle is assured. If you find nothing there, search over the village: there may be an elusive thermal at the end of the fields toward Valle, where the pines start. Taxis from the village can also take you to Valle.

## Peñón–Divisadero

One of the most beautiful and impressive flights when you start flying cross-country. Get to the Maguey as described in the [Wall route](#maguey), then follow the south edge of the Mesa to the west.

Top out before passing the gap between the Mesa and the last of the Tres Reyes. On your first visit you'll be calmer flying high, although with the wind and rock formations along the way you can also fly low: the slope always works. The landings look far, but the terrain drops steeply and you can reach them without trouble, as long as you don't keep scratching far below the rim before gliding out.

On the last part of the ridge before the Divisadero (the antennas), climb to pass over the spines. You can go low around the lee side of the last rocks, but there's usually some convergence lift higher up, and it's much more relaxing.

The usual return is the same way back to the Maguey, then on to Valle as described in the Wall route. You can also return along the north face of the Mesa, but it's seldom done: it doesn't always work, and you must be very careful with landings. The first part of that return crosses a very low valley (Santo Tomás de los Plátanos) full of high-voltage cables, and there's a stretch with no landings before the village of Cerro Gordo.

## Peñón–Saucos–Mariposas

A classic of the site. The difficulty is that the terrain rises in that direction, so even on good days with a high cloudbase you often lose height quickly on transitions. The landmarks for this flight are Quintanilla, Saucos and Mariposas (the Butterflies).

This route enters the restricted area above 4,115 m (13,500 ft): see [Airspace](/en/site-info/airspace/).

Start from Sacamecate (see the [Peñitas route](#sacamecate)). Take as much height as possible there and look closely at where the convergence is. There are two ways to Saucos:

1. **The hills on the right (south) side of the Quintanilla valley**, looking toward Saucos from Sacamecate. They usually work, but it isn't the most direct route, and where the hills end you reach flat terrain with few thermals near Mesa de Dolores (mind the [military base](/en/site-info/airspace/#prohibited-landing-zones) there). If you have enough height to reach the top of the ridge that runs to the right (south-southwest) of the Mariposas, you can usually follow it all the way there.
2. **The hills on the left (north) side of the Quintanilla valley.** The high-voltage cables there usually trigger a thermal: climb well, and you can fly toward Saucos without trouble, or take climbs along the way (there are often several).

At Saucos there are several thermal sources, but the safest are the hills on the left (north) side. Climb well there and you can go on to the Mariposas.

**Beware of the fields just before the Mariposas:** they're Christmas tree plantations. They look like good fields, but they're full of cut stumps.

The return is usually difficult even though the terrain descends, because you have a headwind. With good height to cross the Jovan valley, you can usually reach the dump (Basurero), Sacamecate or San Agustín, get back into the convergence and fly on to Valle. Congratulations if you made it to the Mariposas!
